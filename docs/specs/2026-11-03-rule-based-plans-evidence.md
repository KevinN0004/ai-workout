# Rule-based plans, evidence review

Researched on 2026-11-03 from guidelines, position stands, meta-analyses and trials, for
`docs/specs/2026-11-03-rule-based-plans-design.md`. The spec adopts most of it, and its Decisions
section says where the owner chose otherwise. Rules marked Convention or Inference here are
reasonable defaults, not findings.

## Summary: rebuild the plan rules around twice-weekly exposure

The draft generator in `docs/specs/2026-11-03-rule-based-plans-design.md` already matches the guidelines on its biggest choices: full-body training for 1 to 3 days, upper/lower or push/pull/legs splits above that, moderate rep ranges, and a deterministic library filtered by equipment and level. Five rules conflict with what WHO, ACSM and the clinical guidelines say. **(1) Several goal and focus swaps cut a muscle group to once a week**, below the "all major muscle groups on 2 or more days" floor that WHO 2020 and ACSM 2026 share. **(2) The mobility and recovery tracks prescribe no strength training at all.** **(3) The slot formula gives a 30-minute full-body day only a squat and a hinge**, with no push or pull. **(4) The fat-loss and cardio prescriptions use light, short-rest lifting that has no fat-loss or endurance evidence behind it**, while the plans as a whole deliver well under the 150-minute weekly aerobic minimum. **(5) The strict injury rule removes the very exercise every back, knee, hip, shoulder and elbow guideline names as first-line care.** Because it matches keywords as substrings (checked in the code), it also strips leg presses, calf raises, Pallof presses and leg curls from users whose injury those exercises never load. The fixes are mostly small numeric changes: a per-track time budget, an effort cue (reps in reserve), longer rest for heavy sets, ramp-up warm-up sets, and an invariant the existing quality gate can assert. Four changes are the owner's to decide because they change scope: replacing exclusion with "modify", adding a short health screen, adding a week counter for deloads, and reading age, which the planner form does not collect. Much of the specific dosing below is practitioner convention or inference, and each place where it is says so. The headline 2026 ACSM position stand was seen only through ACSM's own press materials, because the paper is paywalled.

## The guidelines agree on a short list of hard numbers

The adult floor is the same in every major source: **150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, or 75 to 150 of vigorous, plus muscle-strengthening that works all major muscle groups on 2 or more days** ([WHO 2020, Bull et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7719906/); [US PAG 2018](https://health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Physical_Activity_Guidelines_2nd_edition.pdf)). ACSM's 2026 resistance-training position stand is an overview of 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants. Its public summary opens with the same rule ("train all major muscle groups at least 2 days per week and build gradually over time"). It sets **about 10 sets per muscle group per week for hypertrophy and heavier loads (about 80% of 1RM) for 2 to 3 sets per exercise for strength**. It also says that training to failure, machines versus free weights, and complex periodisation "did not consistently change results for the average healthy adult" ([ACSM 2026 infographic](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf); [ACSM press page](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/)). The full text returned HTTP 402, so these figures come from ACSM's own press materials and nothing more granular was verified. The 2009 ACSM progression stand is superseded as headline guidance, but it is still the only source with explicit level tables: **novices 2 to 3 days a week at 8 to 12 RM, intermediates 3 to 4, advanced 4 to 5; 1 to 2 minutes of rest for hypertrophy, 3 to 5 minutes for heavy strength work; a 2 to 10% load increase once the lifter can do 1 to 2 reps more than the target** ([ACSM 2009, PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)).

The meta-analytic literature fills in the shape of the dose-response curve. In a 2026 Bayesian meta-analysis of 67 studies, **frequency added nothing to hypertrophy once weekly volume was equal, but kept a positive, diminishing effect on strength**, and weekly set volume raised both outcomes with diminishing returns ([Pelland et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)). The Springer page redirected to login, so its marginal-effect numbers were not verified. A 2023 network meta-analysis found that every prescription beat no exercise. The top-ranked strength prescription was heavy, multi-set training 3 times a week, and the top hypertrophy prescription was heavy, multi-set training twice a week ([Currier et al., BJSM 2023](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)). For the time-limited general public, a review sets a defensible minimum of **at least 4 weekly sets per muscle in the 6 to 15 RM range, including at least one leg, one push and one pull exercise**, and finds that supersets roughly halve session time at similar volume ([Iversen et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)).

Load and effort matter more than rep-range dogma. Hypertrophy did not differ across loads from 8 RM up to more than 15 RM when sets were taken hard, but strength gains favoured high and moderate loads over light ones ([Lopez et al. 2021](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929)). Training to failure gave at most a trivial hypertrophy edge (ES 0.19) ([Refalo et al. 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760/full)), and **strength gains were similar across reps-in-reserve ranges** ([Robinson et al. 2024](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/)). The consequence for a generator is that a rep range without an effort target is underspecified: "3 x 10" done with a 20RM load is not the prescribed stimulus. Rest shows a similar plateau. A Bayesian meta-analysis found **a small hypertrophy advantage for rests longer than 60 seconds and nothing more past 90**, from 9 studies, mostly in untrained people, with wide intervals ([Singer et al. 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)).

No NSCA primary source was retrieved in this research, so this report says nothing on NSCA's authority. Warm-up durations often attributed to NSCA's _Essentials_ (5 to 10 minutes general plus dynamic work) were not verified and are not used below.

## Day swaps and short sessions break the twice-a-week floor

Most of the weekly structure in the draft is sound. Full-body A/B for 1 to 3 days matches ACSM's novice recommendation of total-body training 2 to 3 days a week ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)). Upper/lower at 4 days and push/pull/legs at 6 days both train each muscle twice a week. The weekday table also avoids back-to-back sessions at 2 and 3 days. The problems are in the substitutions. Applying the draft's own swap rules to its own tables gives these weeks. **A fat-loss or cardio goal at 4 days** becomes upper, lower, conditioning, lower, so the upper body is trained once. **The same goal at 6 or 7 days** turns the fourth day, a push day, into conditioning, so pushing happens once. **A Mobility or Recovery focus** turns the last strength day into mobility, which leaves lower body once at 4 and 5 days and legs once at 6. **The two together at 4 days** give upper, lower, conditioning, mobility: each half of the body trained once. Every one of these weeks falls under the 2-day floor that WHO and ACSM state with high confidence, and once a week is exactly the comparison where a volume-equated meta-analysis found twice weekly better for hypertrophy ([Schoenfeld et al. 2016, via Henselmans' review](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/)). The fix is mechanical. When a swap would leave a pattern with one exposure, the surviving strength day of that pair becomes full body (for example, 4 days for fat loss becomes upper, lower, conditioning, full body). The quality gate can then assert, for every plan with 2 or more strength days, that lower-body, push and pull work each appear on at least 2 days.

The mobility and recovery _tracks_ go further and make every training day a mobility day, so a user who picks "Mobility" or "Recovery" as a goal gets no strength training. That is below the guideline floor, and it is also unnecessary. A meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (n = 452) found **full-range strength training improves range of motion as much as stretching** ([Afonso et al. 2021](https://estudogeral.uc.pt/handle/10316/104612)). The full text was not read, so participant ages and protocols are unconfirmed. Recovery is best modelled as reduced-volume training plus easy aerobic work, not inactivity. Active recovery reduces soreness by small to large amounts ([Dupuy et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00403/pdf)). And the one deload RCT found that **a full week off mid-programme cost lower-body strength gains compared with training through** ([Coleman et al. 2024](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)).

Session length is the second structural gap. The draft fills `clamp(floor((minutes - 10) / 8), 2, 8)` slots. Worked through, that gives **2 exercises at every duration up to 33 minutes, 3 at 34 to 41, and 4 at the default 45**. Because the full-body slot order is squat, hinge, horizontal push, horizontal pull, a 30-minute full-body day contains only a squat and a hinge, and a 40-minute day has no pull. That misses Iversen's leg-push-pull minimum. The constant 10 is also correct only for lean strength (5-minute warm-up plus 5-minute finisher). Fat loss (10-minute finisher) and cardio (15-minute finisher) overfill their sessions by about one exercise. Rough arithmetic from ACSM's 1-to-2-second tempo plus prescribed rest and a transition puts a lean-strength exercise at about 6 to 7 minutes, a heavy 3 x 5 at about 10 (with warm-up sets), and a 3 x 12-15 at 45 seconds' rest at about 5. That is a derivation, not a measured figure. Reordering full body as squat, horizontal push, hinge, horizontal pull, single-leg, core, arms follows ACSM's "multi-joint before single-joint, large before small" rule ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)). It also makes the first four slots cover all quadrants and makes the two main lifts one lower and one upper.

Level is not yet used to bound frequency. A beginner can pick 6 days and receive 6 strength days, double ACSM 2009's novice band ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)). The overtraining consensus describes non-functional overreaching (fatigue, a performance drop, mood disturbance that takes weeks to clear) as the cost of ignoring the training-recovery balance, and notes that no single marker diagnoses it ([Meeusen et al. 2013](https://sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/Meeusen-et-al-2013-Overtraining-Consensus-ECSS-ACSM.pdf)). Capping strength days by level (beginner 3, or 4 as upper/lower; intermediate 4; advanced 6) and turning the extra days into conditioning, easy aerobic or mobility days keeps the user's chosen day count. Those caps are inference from ACSM's bands, at low-to-moderate confidence. No source found mandates a complete rest day at 7 days, and no meta-analysis addresses 7 resistance days a week in general populations.

## Fat loss and cardio need heavier lifting and more walking

The draft gives fat loss 3 x 12-15 at 45 seconds' rest and cardio 2 x 15-20 at 30 seconds. Neither convention has evidence behind it. ACSM's weight-management stand says **resistance training "does not enhance weight loss but may increase fat-free mass"**. It finds 150 to 250 minutes a week of moderate activity gives only modest loss, **more than 250 minutes is associated with clinically significant loss**, and activity helps with moderate but not severe diet restriction ([Donnelly et al., ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19127177/)). Combined aerobic and resistance training reduces fat mass more than resistance alone, and resistance training adds lean mass ([Schwingshackl et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3866267)). In a deficit, then, the job of lifting is to keep muscle and strength. Strength depends on load ([Lopez 2021](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929); [Currier 2023](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)), so main lifts belong in a moderate range (8 to 12) with 90 seconds of rest, and the high-rep, short-rest work goes to accessories. For cardio-focused users, strength training improves running economy by about 2 to 8% ([Blagrove et al. 2018](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Effects_of_strength_training_on_the_physiological_determinants_of_middle-_and_long-distance_running_performance_a_systematic_review/9619553)), and the evidence favours heavy and plyometric work over light, high-rep lifting. That literature is in trained runners, so applying it to recreational users is inference.

On intervals, **HIIT and moderate continuous training produce the same fat loss, with HIIT taking about 40% less time** ([Wewege et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28401638/)). The often-quoted "28.5% more fat loss with intervals" comes from Viana et al. 2019, which BJSM **retracted in December 2020** for data-extraction and effect-size errors ([BJSM retraction](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/10/655); [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2020/12/18/i-dropped-the-ball-magic-bullet-falls-short-of-target)). It must not be cited, in the spec or in coach-note copy. All-out Wingate-style intervals "may not be safe or practical" for the general public. The practical model runs at 80 to 95% of maximum heart rate, for example 10 x 1 minute ([Gibala & Little](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3381816)), so the app should never label its 40/20 rounds "Tabata", which implies all-out work. Even in cardiac rehabilitation, high-intensity intervals had low absolute event rates (2 non-fatal arrests in 46,364 hours) ([Rognmo 2012, via ACC](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/journal-scans/2012/08/14/21/25/cardiovascular-risk-of-high-versus-moderate-intensity-aerobic-exercise)). Limiting hard sessions to 2 a week for beginners, on non-consecutive days, is practitioner convention; no study fixes the number. Interference is small: adding aerobic work did not compromise hypertrophy or maximal strength across 43 studies, only explosive strength when both were done in the same session ([Schumann et al. 2022](https://portfolio.jcu.edu.au/publications/JCU174136)). The draft's "lifts first, finisher last" order is therefore fine. The one placement worth avoiding is hard running the day before a lower-body day, which is inference from running-specific interference ([Wilson et al. 2012](https://www.pubmed.gov/22002517/)).

The aerobic total is where the plans fall shortest. Under a generous accounting (every interval minute counted as vigorous, so double), a 3-day plan's finishers give about 60 moderate-equivalent minutes for fat loss, 45 for cardio and 15 for lean strength. **That is 10 to 40% of the 150-minute floor.** Lifting counts as muscle-strengthening, not aerobic activity. The rest days are where the gap closes. The guidelines no longer require 10-minute bouts, so all activity counts ([Harvard summary of PAG 2018](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/?p=15239)). A brisk walk is checkable without a wearable: **about 100 steps a minute is moderate intensity** ([Tudor-Locke et al. 2018](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/12/776)), and the talk test tracks the ventilatory thresholds ([Sports Med 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01690-3)). On a 3-day lean-strength plan the finishers give about 15 minutes, so three 45-minute brisk walks on rest days (leaving one day free) bring the week to about 150. A 3-day fat-loss plan with 45-minute walks on all four rest days reaches about 240, close to ACSM's 250. A fixed 30-minute walk is not enough for lean strength: four of them total about 135 minutes. The walk length should therefore be computed from the gap to each track's target, not fixed. That arithmetic is the report's own. One correction to common copy: the "10% rule" for adding running volume failed in a 532-runner RCT (injury rates 20.8% versus 20.3%) ([Buist et al. 2008](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-r/)), so coach notes should not present it as an injury rule.

## The strict injury rule removes the guidelines' first-line treatment

On this decision the clinical evidence is unusually consistent. For chronic low back pain, a Cochrane review of 249 trials found exercise probably reduces pain, by 15.2 points out of 100 against no treatment or usual care, with adverse-event rates similar to comparison groups (33% versus 29%) ([Hayden et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8477273)). The WHO's 2023 guideline recommends structured exercise ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/07-12-2023-who-releases-guidelines-on-chronic-low-back-pain)), and ACP puts it first-line ([Qaseem et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28192789/)). In an RCT, **supervised deadlift training performed about as well as low-load motor-control exercise** for mechanical low back pain ([NCT01061632](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01061632)), and lumbar flexion while lifting is not a risk factor for back pain (low-quality evidence) ([Saraceni et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8294511)). For knee and hip osteoarthritis, exercise is core in OARSI ([Bannuru 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31278997/)), and it is the one non-drug treatment ACR strongly recommends ([Healio on ACR 2019](https://www.healio.com/news/rheumatology/20191113/exercise-remains-only-nondrug-intervention-for-oa-as-draft-guidelines-progress)). NICE NG226 tells clinicians to offer everyone therapeutic exercise and to warn that pain may rise at first ([NICE NG226](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations)). Patellofemoral pain is treated first with combined hip and knee strengthening ([Willy et al., JOSPT 2019](https://www.pubmed.gov/31475628/)). For rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain, only _progressive, resisted_ exercise showed benefit; non-progressive or non-resisted exercise showed none (low certainty) ([Naunton et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32571081/)). For lateral elbow tendinopathy, active strengthening is first-line ([Karanasios et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/9/477)). For gluteal tendinopathy, education plus exercise beat a steroid injection ([LEAP, Mellor 2018](https://wikiMSK.org/w/img_auth.php/4/46/Mellor2018_-_Leap_Trial.pdf)). Where they address it, these sources describe dosing to symptoms: in the Achilles pain-monitoring model, pain up to 5/10 was allowed if it settled by the next morning and did not rise week to week ([Silbernagel et al. 2007](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/18389)). Across 7 RCTs, exercise that allowed some pain did no worse than pain-free exercise ([Smith et al. 2017](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/41764/)).

The strict rule does the opposite. The knee keywords (`jump`, `lunge`, `pistol`, `squat`, `step-up`) remove every squat and most single-leg entries. A knee user's lower day falls to hinge, glutes-calves and core: almost none of the loaded knee flexion that OARSI, ACR, NICE and the JOSPT guideline treat as the therapy. The hip keywords remove squat, hinge and lunge together. The code also makes the false positives certain rather than hypothetical. The keyword lists in `recommendationUtils.js` match by `.includes()` substring, and so does the scorer the gate uses (`scorePlan.mjs`, which counts any plan line containing a flagged keyword as an injury hit). So `press` excludes leg press, floor press and Pallof press for a shoulder or wrist/elbow flag. `raise` excludes calf and leg raises for a shoulder. `curl` and `extension` exclude leg curls, leg extensions and back extensions for the wrist and elbow. `kickback` excludes triceps kickbacks for a hip, and `jump` excludes jump rope for a knee. The spec's library test ("no entry's name holds a keyword of an injury its `stresses` leaves out") would force those entries to be _tagged_ as stressing the unrelated joint. That builds the false positives into the library itself.

The evidence-concordant alternative stays deterministic. Each library entry carries `ok`, `modify` or `avoid` for each injury, and only `avoid` is excluded. A `modify` entry is replaced by its regression, with a cue attached: a box squat to a comfortable height or a wall sit for a knee; a hip thrust, then a dumbbell Romanian deadlift to mid-shin for a back, at accessory loads rather than 4 x 5; neutral-grip and handle push-ups for a wrist; band external rotation added for a shoulder. A small `avoid` list covers the high-irritability or poorly scalable lifts: jumps and pistols for a knee, the heavy barbell deadlift as a main lift for a back, upright rows, behind-the-neck work, dips, snatches and jerks for a shoulder, dips and skull-crushers for an elbow. Those variant choices are biomechanical reasoning and clinical convention, not guideline text: no source found grades individual gym lifts for any of these conditions. Whatever the owner decides on strictness, keyword matching should apply only to free-text injuries. Curated entries should be judged by their tags.

## Warm-ups, recovery and safety text need specific, sourced wording

Warm-ups help more often than they hurt: performance improved in 79% of the outcome measures in a meta-analysis ([Fradkin et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996770/)). For lifting, the part with evidence is the _specific_ warm-up. In trained men, sets close to the working load optimised squat and bench performance, and "few repetitions and low loads" were not enough ([Ribeiro et al. 2021](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360298452769458688?lang=en)). The evidence is small and partly conflicting, and none of it comes from untrained people. So the draft's 5-minute general warm-up is a reasonable floor that needs ramp-up sets of the first main lift after it. The 50/70/85-90% ladder suggested below is coaching convention. Static stretches under 30 seconds have no measurable cost to performance, and the losses become clear past 60 seconds ([Kay & Blazevich 2012](https://www.anatomytrains.com/wp-content/uploads/manual/meta-analysis.pdf); [Behm et al. 2016](https://nectar.northampton.ac.uk/id/eprint/8154/)). ACSM's flexibility dose is **about 60 seconds in total per stretch on 2 or more days a week, in holds of 10 to 30 seconds for most adults and 30 to 60 seconds for older adults** ([Garber et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/); [ACSM 2021 update](https://www.acsm.org/blog-detail/acsm-certified-blog/2021/03/18/stretching-and-flexibility-guidelines-update)). The draft's 2 x 45 seconds is therefore generous but harmless on a stand-alone mobility day, and 2 x 30 seconds is the evidence-matched default. **WHO adds balance-and-strength work on 3 or more days for everyone 65 and over** ([WHO 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7719906/)), and the draft has no balance slot at all.

Deload evidence is thin. A Delphi of expert coaches defined a deload as "a period of reduced training stress", not a stop ([Bell et al. 2023](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417/1/s40798-023-00633-0.pdf)). Competitive strength athletes self-report deloading for about 6.4 days every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks ([Rogerson et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/7d15e260e0ac4cff9e8c685dd7335389)), and the only RCT found that stopping entirely cost strength ([Coleman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)). "Half the sets, same load, every 5th or 6th week" is a reasonable convention, but the generator cannot apply it without knowing which week a plan is. Sleep advice can be sourced directly: adults should sleep **7 or more hours a night** ([AASM/SRS consensus](https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf)).

On screening, ACSM's current algorithm asks about three things: whether the person already exercises, known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and symptoms. It routes people to "start light-to-moderate and progress" or to medical clearance ([Whitfield, Riebe et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)). Applied to US adults over 40, it would refer 54.2% of them before any exercise, mostly on self-reported breathlessness, which argues for advising rather than blocking. The PAR-Q+ is the validated self-screen, but its licence says "NO changes are permitted", so an app must link to the whole form or write its own items ([PAR-Q+ 2025](https://southlake.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/PARQPlus2025-Form-fillable.pdf)). The draft's fallback injury note, "stop anything that hurts", conflicts with the pain-monitoring evidence above. Clinicians' avoidance-framed explanations are a recognised source of nocebo and fear-avoidance effects, though that argument comes from an opinion piece and has not been tested in apps ([Cormack & Rossettini 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10701680/)). Regulators draw the line at claims. The FDA's General Wellness policy, reissued in January 2026, excludes products that diagnose, treat or "mitigate" a condition or recommend clinical thresholds ([Troutman summary](https://troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices)). The EU's MDCG 2019-11 likewise turns on medical intended purpose ([OpenRegulatory](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2019-11)). So notes should never promise injury treatment, and the Gemini prompt should forbid it.

## Rule-by-rule recommendations

Evidence strength key: **High** means a guideline or a meta-analysis of RCTs. **Moderate** means a single RCT, a consensus or Delphi, or a narrative review. **Low** means small or indirect studies. **Convention** means practitioner practice with no direct test. **Inference** means the report's own arithmetic or reasoning. Numbers marked Convention or Inference are reasonable defaults, not findings.

## Decisions only the owner can make

Six choices change the product's scope rather than its numbers, so the evidence can inform them but not settle them. **First, strict versus modify.** The evidence favours modify. Strict is defensible as a liability posture for an unsupervised app, but it should be adopted knowing that it withholds the guidelines' recommended treatment and, through substring matching, removes exercises unrelated to the injury. Either way, the scorer and the library test must change with it. **Second, a health screen.** Three to five yes/no items in the app's own words (known heart, metabolic or kidney disease; chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness; told to exercise only under supervision; pregnant or recently postpartum; optionally an age band) would let the rules cap intensity at light-to-moderate, drop vigorous finishers and show the right referral line, without blocking the plan. This touches the planner form, which the spec lists as out of scope. The PAR-Q+ cannot be edited or abbreviated under its licence, and no validation exists for any shortened app screen. **Third, deloads need a week counter.** The generator is a pure function of the form, so a deload requires either an input such as "weeks since the plan started" or a count of saved plans, which sits naturally with roadmap item B4's four-week history. **Fourth, age.** The planner form (`createDefaultPlannerForm` in `client/src/app/constants.js`) has no age field. The personal profile form does carry `age`, but `generateRoutes.js` makes no reference to it. Only those two places were checked, so whether the route could read the profile elsewhere is unconfirmed. Age gates the WHO 65+ balance rule, the longer stretch holds, the PAR-Q+ "over 45 and new to vigorous exercise" line, and an under-18 exclusion. Without it, the fallback is to put a balance slot on every mobility day for everyone. **Fifth, level caps.** Converting a beginner's fourth to seventh days into easy days honours the chosen day count but not the expectation of more lifting. **Sixth, prescribed rest-day walks.** These change what a "rest day" means. Planned-versus-done counting (P6) should be checked for how it treats a rest day that carries a walk; that check was not done here.

## Where the evidence is thin, paywalled or retracted

Several load-bearing sources were seen only partially, and the owner should weight the rules that depend on them accordingly. The **ACSM 2026 stand** was read only through ACSM's press page and infographic and through news coverage. Its evidence grades, its level-specific advice and any rest-interval figures are unknown. **Pelland 2026's** per-set marginal effects, **Afonso 2021's** participant details, **Bell 2023's** recommendations beyond its definition of a deload, and **how Rogerson 2024's athletes reduced load** were not verified. **NICE NG59** (low back pain) returned HTTP 403 and is not cited. **ACR's** per-exercise grades, **ACOG 804** and the **Canadian pregnancy guideline** were seen through secondary summaries. **ACSM's 2015 screening paper** was read through a 2017 paper by the same group. **No NSCA source** was retrieved. The **Viana 2019** interval meta-analysis is retracted and must not be relied on anywhere. The **Murphy and Koehler** 500 kcal threshold has a published critique. **Saraceni 2020** is low-quality evidence. The **5/10 pain ceiling** comes from Achilles tendinopathy, and the "3/10" band, the 48-hour recovery rule, McGill's core dosing and the ramp-up percentages are convention with no source found. No source tested app warning wording, exclusion messaging or shortened screens against injuries or adherence. All of the per-exercise minute constants, the day sequences and the weekly aerobic arithmetic are this report's own derivations.

## Conclusion

The draft's weaknesses are not in its rep ranges, which the evidence treats as broadly interchangeable. They are in its edge cases: swaps, short sessions, non-strength goals and injuries, where a deterministic rule quietly drops a muscle group, a whole training mode or the therapeutic exercise itself. That suggests a different use for the quality gate. It currently asserts what a plan must _not_ contain (equipment the user lacks, injury keywords). The more valuable assertions are what every plan _must_ contain: twice-weekly lower, push and pull exposure, a leg-push-pull minimum in every full-body day, a weekly aerobic total that reaches the guideline floor, and an `ok` regression for every flagged pattern. Those are guideline-level rules (High evidence), checkable across all 12,000 generated plans. Encoding them as invariants protects the plans against future library edits better than any single prescription number.

The second shift is in what "safe" means for an unsupervised app. Clinically, the safety-critical content is a short symptom list (chest pain, fainting, disproportionate breathlessness, palpitations, and the cauda equina signs for backs), proportionate screening, and a conservative start. It is not the removal of squats from knees. A design that always appends rule-written red-flag text, caps intensity when a screen answer warrants it, and swaps rather than deletes movements would sit closer to ACSM, WHO and NICE than the strict rule does. It would also keep the generator pure, deterministic and fully testable, which was the reason for building the generator in the first place.
